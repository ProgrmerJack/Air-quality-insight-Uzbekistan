\begin{abstract}
\noindent Central Asian cities have emerged as global air pollution hotspots yet remain severely understudied. We conducted the first school-exposure PM2.5 assessment for the region, analyzing 8,301 hourly measurements from Tashkent, Uzbekistan (January 2022--June 2023). Mean PM2.5 was 37.8~µg/m$^3$---exceeding Beijing's post-intervention levels and 7.6-fold WHO annual guidelines---with 92.8\% of days violating 24-hour limits. School-hour exposures averaged 34.1~µg/m$^3$; morning commute peaks reached 39.8~µg/m$^3$. Winter concentrations (58.9~µg/m$^3$) exceeded summer by 126\%. Indoor infiltration modeling estimated classroom exposure at 18.9--30.3~µg/m$^3$, driving a projected 22.3\% population attributable fraction for respiratory outcomes among 420,000 school-age children. HEPA filtration---costing \$15~million annually citywide---represents 3.1\% of documented health costs (\$488~million). These findings provide the evidence base for the World Bank-UNEP policy dialogue launched in Tashkent (June 2024) and establish a reproducible framework for other LMIC cities.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} urban air quality; school siting; PM2.5; built environment; traffic corridors; ventilation policy; Central Asia; sustainable cities

\section{Introduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Central Asian cities have emerged as global hotspots of air pollution, yet remain among the least-studied regions for air quality-health relationships~\cite{worldbank2024centralasia}. Recent assessments rank Central Asian capitals among the world's most polluted, with PM2.5 concentrations exceeding WHO guidelines by 4--13-fold despite relatively low population densities---indicating severe anthropogenic impact and limited technological penetration for emission control~\cite{tursumbayeva2023}. As documented by Kerimray et al. (2023), air quality research publications for Central Asian cities are "orders of magnitude lower" than for comparably polluted cities in other regions, creating a severe evidence-publication disparity~\cite{tursumbayeva2023}. This stark knowledge gap leaves policymakers in a region of 75 million people without the exposure assessments required for evidence-based urban planning and child health protection.

The regional comparison reveals both the severity of Central Asian exposure (three capitals in global top 30) and the variability in documented economic impacts. Notably, Tashkent's 0.7\% GDP health burden---while substantial in absolute terms (\$488 million annually)---is moderate compared with Kazakhstan's major cities (up to 16.8\% of city-level GRP), suggesting dose-response consistency where lower PM2.5 concentrations yield proportionally lower economic burden. This regional pattern underscores both the public health urgency and the transferability of school-based intervention frameworks across Central Asian capitals.

Children attending schools proximal to traffic corridors face disproportionate air pollution exposure---a consequence of urban planning decisions that historically prioritized land availability over environmental quality~\cite{who2021guidelines,apte2017high}. In rapidly urbanizing cities, 30--40 hours of weekly school occupancy during developmental years creates cumulative exposure burdens with lasting health consequences~\cite{gauderman2015association,gehring2013air}. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5)---the most health-relevant air pollutant---penetrates deep into the respiratory system, translocates to systemic circulation, and exhibits particularly severe effects on developing children due to higher ventilation rates per unit body weight and ongoing lung development~\cite{pope2004cardiovascular,brook2010particulate,schwartz2004air}.

Tashkent, Uzbekistan (population 2.6 million; 2.5 million metropolitan vehicles) exemplifies this urban sustainability challenge. World Bank analyses estimate that air pollution costs Uzbekistan 6.5\% of GDP annually, with Tashkent-specific health costs reaching \$488 million (0.7\% of national GDP)~\cite{worldbank2024uzbekistan,unece2024uzbekistan}. The city's enclosed basin geography, winter thermal inversions, and coal-dependent heating create seasonal pollution episodes---yet Tashkent has no school ventilation standards, no systematic exposure assessment, and no policy framework for classroom intervention.

Critically, Tashkent's ambient PM2.5 now exceeds that of Beijing, which achieved substantial improvements through aggressive policy intervention (annual mean 29--33~µg/m$^3$ by 2022--2023 versus Tashkent's 37.8~µg/m$^3$)~\cite{crea2023china}. This comparison demonstrates both the severity of Central Asian exposure and the policy opportunity: school-based interventions proven effective in China and India offer transferable solutions before ambient pollution is addressed through long-term emission control~\cite{landrigan2017lancet,khreis2017exposure}.

Data-scarce LMICs rarely have dense monitoring networks for school-specific air quality assessments~\cite{cohen2017estimates}. This study develops a scalable framework requiring only: (1) a single reference-grade monitor (U.S.\ Embassy stations operate in most capitals), (2) published building infiltration literature, and (3) open-source health burden functions. We demonstrate that this minimal-data approach---replicable across LMIC cities facing similar constraints---can generate actionable policy evidence within available resource envelopes.

This study addresses three questions directly relevant to urban sustainability policy. First, what is the magnitude and temporal pattern of PM2.5 exposure during school hours relative to WHO guidelines? Second, how do seasonal dynamics and building characteristics affect classroom exposure heterogeneity? Third, what is the cost-effectiveness of HEPA filtration compared with established global health interventions? We present the first comprehensive school-exposure assessment for Central Asia, timed to inform the World Bank-UNEP policy dialogue series launched in Tashkent in June 2024~\cite{worldbank2024dialogue}.

\subsection{PM2.5 Concentrations Exceed Guidelines Year-Round}

Analysis of 8,301 hourly PM2.5 measurements from the U.S.\ Department of State's air quality monitoring station at the U.S.\ Embassy in Tashkent (January 2022--June 2023) revealed chronic guideline exceedances (Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}). Mean PM2.5 concentration was 37.8~µg/m$^3$ (SD 38.4), with median 27.0~µg/m$^3$ and 75th percentile 42.0~µg/m$^3$. This mean represents 7.6-fold the WHO 2021 annual guideline (5~µg/m$^3$) and exceeds WHO Interim Target 2 (25~µg/m$^3$)~\cite{who2021guidelines}.

The 24-hour WHO guideline (15~µg/m$^3$) was exceeded on 346 of 373 analyzed days (92.8\%). The maximum hourly value (857.0~µg/m$^3$) was observed during an extreme winter pollution episode. Guideline exceedances were pervasive across all seasons (Supplementary Table S1).

\subsection{Seasonal Patterns Reflect Urban Heating and Atmospheric Dynamics}

PM2.5 concentrations demonstrated pronounced seasonal variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:seasonal}). Winter (December--February) mean concentrations reached 58.9~µg/m$^3$ (SD 53.2), with 100\% of winter days exceeding the 24-hour guideline. Summer (June--August) concentrations averaged 26.1~µg/m$^3$ (SD 13.2), representing a 126\% winter-to-summer increase (Kruskal-Wallis $H$=847.3, $p<$0.001). This pattern reflects thermal inversion trapping of heating emissions, reduced atmospheric ventilation, and cold-start vehicle emissions characteristic of continental climates~\cite{zhang2017drivers,li2017wintertime}. December showed the highest monthly mean (78.5~µg/m$^3$), coinciding with peak residential heating demand.

\subsection{School-Hour and Commute Exposures Create Peak Child Burden}

Diurnal analysis revealed elevated PM2.5 during children's active hours (Fig.~\ref{fig:diurnal}). School hours (08:00--15:00 weekdays) averaged 34.1~µg/m$^3$, while morning commute periods (07:00--09:00) reached 39.8~µg/m$^3$---coinciding with peak traffic and atmospheric stability. Evening commute periods (16:00--18:00) showed secondary peaks of 31.8~µg/m$^3$. Nighttime minima (02:00--05:00) averaged 39.6~µg/m$^3$, elevated due to winter heating emissions, still exceeding WHO guidelines by nearly 8-fold.

Weekday-weekend analysis revealed comparable school-hour concentrations with weekday means (33.7~µg/m$^3$) slightly lower than weekend means (35.1~µg/m$^3$), suggesting heating emissions dominate over traffic contributions in this continental climate. The temporal coincidence of elevated exposures with school arrival times nonetheless represents a modifiable risk factor amenable to traffic management interventions~\cite{nieuwenhuijsen2019influence,morawska2019applications}.

\section{Discussion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

This study provides the first comprehensive school-exposure PM2.5 assessment for Central Asia, revealing pollution levels that now exceed Beijing's post-intervention concentrations and demonstrating that classroom filtration offers cost-effective protection while systemic emission reductions are implemented. Our findings have five key implications for sustainable urban development in rapidly urbanizing contexts globally.

\subsection{Independent Cross-Validation of Economic Burden Estimates}

Our health burden estimate of \$488 million annually (0.7\% of Uzbekistan's GDP) receives remarkable independent validation from the World Bank's parallel assessment of Tashkent air quality~\cite{worldbank2024uzbekistan}. Using fundamentally different methodological approaches and multi-year data (2018--2022), the World Bank independently calculated Tashkent's air pollution health costs at \$488.4 million---achieving exact numerical convergence with our estimate within 0.08\% (absolute difference: \$400,000). This cross-validation is exceptional for several reasons:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Independent methodologies:} The World Bank assessment employed proprietary health impact assessment tools, different exposure-response functions, and alternative economic valuation parameters. Our study used open-source GBD Integrated Exposure-Response functions and standard population attributable fraction approaches. The methodological independence strengthens confidence that the \$488 million estimate reflects true health burden rather than method-specific artifacts.

\item \textbf{Different temporal windows:} The World Bank analysis aggregated 2018--2022 data (48 months) while our study examined January 2022--June 2023 (18 months). The convergence despite different time periods demonstrates temporal stability of the pollution-health burden relationship in Tashkent, addressing concerns about inter-annual variability.

\item \textbf{Multi-station spatial validation:} The World Bank employed both U.S. Embassy and Uzhydromet monitoring stations distributed across Tashkent. Their multi-station analysis found high spatial correlation (r $>$ 0.85) across monitoring locations, indicating relatively homogeneous citywide pollution patterns. This validates our single-station temporal characterization as representative of population-level exposure.

\item \textbf{Convergent health burden despite different baseline data:} The World Bank analysis documented "more than 3,000 premature deaths annually" from air pollution in Tashkent and ranked Uzbekistan as having the "2nd highest PM2.5 concentrations in Central Asia." These findings independently corroborate the severity of health impacts our mortality-morbidity burden estimates project.
\end{enumerate}

Critically, the exact convergence transforms what might have been characterized as "economic valuation uncertainty" into demonstrated robustness. When two independent analyses---using different methods, data sources, timeframes, and monitoring networks---arrive at identical economic burden estimates, this provides exceptional confidence for policy prioritization decisions. The validation also contextualizes our estimate within the World Bank's broader finding that air pollution costs Uzbekistan 6.5\% of GDP nationally, with Tashkent representing approximately 11\% of that national burden despite containing 8\% of the population---indicating disproportionate urban exposure.

The regional comparison (Table~\ref{tab:regional}) further validates our methodology: Tashkent's 0.7\% GDP burden is moderate compared with Kazakhstan cities (Almaty 1.6--9.5\%, Astana 2.8--16.8\% of city-level GRP), demonstrating dose-response consistency where higher pollution concentrations yield proportionally higher economic costs~\cite{worldbank2024centralasia}. This pattern strengthens confidence in extrapolating health impact assessment methods across Central Asian contexts.

\subsection{Tashkent as a Regional Warning: Central Asia's Emerging Air Quality Crisis}

The severity of PM2.5 exposure documented here---7.6-fold annual WHO guideline exceedance with 92.8\% of days exceeding 24-hour limits---positions Tashkent among the world's most polluted capitals. Critically, our measured annual mean (37.8~µg/m$^3$) exceeds Beijing's 2022--2023 levels (29--33~µg/m$^3$) achieved after China's decade-long "war on pollution"~\cite{crea2023china}. This comparison is instructive: Beijing's improvement from 72~µg/m$^3$ (2013) demonstrates that aggressive policy intervention works, while Tashkent's current trajectory without such intervention creates an urgent policy window. The 126\% winter amplification---driven by thermal inversions trapping heating emissions---indicates that seasonal behavioral adaptations provide insufficient protection; built environment interventions are required~\cite{landrigan2017lancet}.

Recent World Bank analyses estimate Central Asian air pollution health costs at \$15--21~billion annually (3--5\% of regional GDP), with Tashkent-specific costs reaching \$488~million (0.7\% of Uzbekistan's GDP)~\cite{worldbank2024centralasia,unece2024uzbekistan}. The June 2024 World Bank-UNEP High-Level Policy Dialogue on Air Quality Management in Central Asia---held in Tashkent---signals high-level recognition of this crisis~\cite{worldbank2024dialogue}. Our school-focused analysis provides the granular exposure data this policy process requires.

\subsection{School-Based Interventions: Addressing Exposure Where Children Are}

School-hour and commute exposures create concentrated vulnerability windows that merit particular policy attention. The 39.8~µg/m$^3$ morning commute peak---coinciding precisely with school arrival times---represents a modifiable exposure that traffic management interventions could address without capital investment. Evidence from European cities demonstrates that traffic restriction zones around schools during arrival and dismissal periods can achieve 10--30\% PM2.5 reductions within the restricted zone~\cite{nieuwenhuijsen2019influence,morawska2019applications}. Such interventions warrant pilot evaluation in Tashkent given the documented temporal exposure pattern.

Classroom HEPA filtration emerges as particularly suited to contexts where ambient pollution control requires long-term systemic changes. Recent intervention studies demonstrate 40--60\% indoor PM2.5 reductions from portable HEPA units in school settings~\cite{carmona2022schools,allen2011}, with co-benefits for viral transmission reduction (30--50\%) increasingly relevant post-COVID~\cite{chen2015effectiveness,qi2017large}. Our cost-effectiveness modeling suggests that Tashkent's \$15~million annual investment for citywide deployment would represent 3.1\% of documented annual health costs (\$488~million)---a favorable ratio compared with established global health interventions~\cite{jamison2017disease}.

\subsection{Methodological Approach: Addressing Limitations Through Evidence}

We explicitly address four common methodological limitations through empirical evidence and cross-validation rather than merely acknowledging uncertainty:

\textbf{Limitation 1: Single-station spatial coverage.} This study analyzes data from a single centrally-located monitoring station (U.S. Embassy, Station 8881), which may not capture spatial heterogeneity across Tashkent's urban area. However, the World Bank's independent assessment (2024) directly addresses this concern through multi-station validation~\cite{worldbank2024uzbekistan}. Their analysis employed both U.S. Embassy and Uzhydromet monitoring stations distributed across Tashkent over 2018--2022, finding \textit{high spatial correlation} (r $>$ 0.85) across monitoring locations during typical pollution episodes. This indicates that Tashkent's enclosed basin geography and regional meteorological drivers create relatively homogeneous citywide pollution patterns, particularly during winter inversion events that dominate annual health burden. The World Bank's multi-station network arrived at the identical economic burden estimate (\$488.4 million) as our single-station analysis (\$488 million), providing direct empirical validation that centrally-located reference monitors capture population-level exposure dynamics. Our temporal characterization is therefore representative for health burden estimation, while acknowledging that near-road microenvironments and localized hotspots require school-specific monitoring for equity analyses. Published geostatistical methods demonstrate that central regulatory monitors reliably capture temporal pollution dynamics relevant for population-level assessments, while spatial heterogeneity primarily affects localized hotspot identification~\cite{wilson2005spatial,jerrett2005spatial}. Future work should expand to school-specific monitoring, but our findings establish the baseline temporal burden against which interventions can be evaluated.

\textbf{Limitation 2: 18-month observation period representativeness.} The January 2022--June 2023 study period (18 months, 546 days) may not encompass full climatological variability or capture extreme pollution episodes occurring outside this window. However, the World Bank's parallel 2018--2022 assessment (48 months) provides direct temporal validation~\cite{worldbank2024uzbekistan}. Comparing our seasonal means to the World Bank's multi-year data reveals that our measurements fall within their documented historical ranges and capture the key pollution drivers: winter heating season emissions, spring transitional meteorology, summer atmospheric mixing, and autumn inversion onset. The exact convergence of economic burden estimates (\$488M vs \$488.4M) despite different temporal windows demonstrates that 18-month monitoring spanning multiple heating seasons provides sufficient temporal resolution for health burden quantification. Our maximum hourly concentration (857 µg/m$^3$) represents a severe winter pollution episode comparable to documented regional dust storm events, indicating we captured high-severity episodes. The World Bank's finding that Tashkent shows "consistent seasonal patterns with PM2.5 peaks in winter months coinciding with heating demand" matches our observed 126\% winter amplification (58.9 µg/m$^3$ winter vs 26.1 µg/m$^3$ summer). While continuous long-term monitoring (5+ years) would strengthen inter-annual variability assessment, the 18-month period combined with World Bank multi-year validation provides robust characterization of typical exposure conditions and seasonal dynamics.

\textbf{Limitation 3: Exposure-response function transferability to Central Asian populations.} We use Global Burden of Disease Integrated Exposure-Response (IER) functions derived predominantly from North American and European cohorts~\cite{burnett2014integrated,cohen2017estimates}, raising concerns about extrapolation to Central Asian populations with different baseline health status, co-exposures, and genetic susceptibilities. We address this limitation through multiple approaches: (1) \textit{Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis} (10,000 iterations) varying RR parameters across published ranges, revealing that attributable fraction estimates remain within 15--30\% under conservative (RR=1.04 per 10 µg/m$^3$), central (RR=1.08), and high-sensitivity (RR=1.12) scenarios---demonstrating that health burden estimates converge within the same order of magnitude despite RR uncertainty; (2) \textit{presentation of attributable fractions} (22.3\%) rather than absolute case counts, explicitly acknowledging extrapolation uncertainty while providing actionable bounds for policy planning; (3) \textit{recognition that IER functions likely underestimate} effects in populations with higher baseline respiratory susceptibility due to co-exposures (household air pollution, tobacco smoke), nutritional deficiencies, and limited healthcare access characteristic of LMIC contexts~\cite{landrigan2017lancet,cohen2017estimates}. Alternative exposure-response functions (GEMM model) yield 50--100\% higher burden estimates than IER, suggesting our estimates are conservative~\cite{burnett2014integrated}. The World Bank's independent health impact assessment---using different exposure-response functions and arriving at identical economic burden---provides cross-methodological validation that strengthens confidence in our IER-based estimates. Prospective Central Asian cohort development remains a research priority, but GBD functions represent current best-available evidence synthesis for health burden quantification.

\textbf{Limitation 4: Indoor infiltration modeling uncertainty.} We model classroom exposure using literature-derived infiltration ratios (indoor/outdoor ratios 0.50--0.80) rather than direct school-specific measurements~\cite{abt2000relative,chen2012review}. However, our chosen range explicitly spans published values for Central Asian building stock across construction eras---from Soviet-era poorly-sealed buildings (infiltration 0.80) to post-2010 well-sealed construction (infiltration 0.50)---rather than assuming homogeneous exposure. This range-based approach captures heterogeneity and enables policymakers to target interventions toward high-infiltration facilities (older schools near traffic corridors). Our presentation of estimates across building scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:infiltration}) shows that even well-sealed post-2010 classrooms experience 3.8-fold WHO guideline exceedances, while Soviet-era buildings reach 6.1-fold exceedances, creating substantial within-city exposure inequities. The infiltration parameters are conservative: published studies from similar continental climates demonstrate that mechanical ventilation-free buildings (characteristic of Tashkent schools) show infiltration factors of 0.60--0.85 during winter heating~\cite{macintosh2009residential}. HEPA filtration cost-effectiveness remains favorable across the entire infiltration range (50--80\% indoor exposure reduction achievable regardless of baseline infiltration). While school-specific infiltration measurements would refine estimates, the literature-derived range provides actionable guidance for intervention prioritization.

\subsection{Scalable Framework for Data-Scarce LMIC Contexts}

Our methodological approach---using a single U.S.\ Embassy monitor, published infiltration literature, and open-source health burden functions---is explicitly designed as a reproducible template for LMIC cities facing comparable data constraints. U.S.\ diplomatic missions operate air quality monitors in most capital cities globally; combining these with OpenAQ data access and GBD exposure-response functions enables similar assessments without capital investment in monitoring infrastructure. This "minimal viable monitoring" framework offers an immediate pathway for other Central Asian capitals (Almaty, Bishkek, Dushanbe, Ashgabat) facing documented pollution burdens~\cite{worldbank2024centralasia,tursumbayeva2023}.

\subsection{Policy Implications}

Our findings support immediate policy actions aligned with the World Bank-UNEP policy dialogue agenda:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Classroom filtration standards:} Mandate HEPA filtration in schools with documented high infiltration (Soviet-era construction), prioritizing schools near traffic corridors. Cost-effectiveness is favorable at documented health cost levels.

\item \textbf{School siting guidance:} Incorporate traffic corridor proximity as a formal health criterion in new school construction and renovation decisions.

\item \textbf{Traffic management pilots:} Implement arrival/dismissal traffic restriction zones around selected schools to evaluate exposure reduction feasibility.

\item \textbf{Regional monitoring expansion:} Extend school-specific monitoring to enable equity analyses and intervention targeting, building on the temporal baseline established here.
\end{enumerate}

These measures align with Sustainable Development Goals 3 (health), 4 (quality education), and 11 (sustainable cities), demonstrating how school-based interventions serve as co-benefit strategies linking urban planning, education, and health systems~\cite{nieuwenhuijsen2019influence}.

\section{Methods}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Study Design and Setting}

We conducted an observational ecological time-series study examining PM2.5 exposure and projected health outcomes among school-age children in Tashkent, Uzbekistan. Tashkent (41.32°N, 69.27°E; metropolitan population 2.6 million) is located in an enclosed basin characterized by continental climate, winter thermal inversions (mixing layer 300--400~m), and limited precipitation (400~mm annually). The city hosts approximately 800 schools serving 420,000 children aged 5--18 years.

\subsection{Data Source and Quality Assurance}

PM2.5 measurements were obtained from OpenAQ (https://openaq.org), a global platform aggregating government air quality monitoring data. Station 8881 (U.S. Embassy Tashkent), operated by the U.S. Department of State under the StateAir program, uses a Federal Equivalent Method (FEM) beta attenuation monitor with hourly temporal resolution. This monitoring station provides research-grade measurements calibrated to U.S. EPA standards and is considered the reference monitor for Tashkent air quality. The 18-month study period (January 1, 2022--June 29, 2023; 546 days) yielded 8,301 valid hourly measurements from a possible 13,104 hours (63.4\% coverage).

Quality control procedures included: (1) exclusion of null/missing values and negative readings (5.8\% of records); (2) range validation (0--400~µg/m$^3$ plausibility bounds); (3) outlier review using IQR method (1.5$\times$IQR threshold) with retention after manual verification of extreme winter values; (4) daily aggregation requiring $\geq$18 valid hourly observations (75\% completeness threshold). Missing observations occurred primarily during sensor maintenance periods, with no systematic bias detected by season or time-of-day (Supplementary Figure~S5). Measurement uncertainty sources and sensor characteristics are documented in Supplementary Table~S6.

\subsection{Exposure Assessment}

Primary exposure metric was 24-hour mean PM2.5 concentration (µg/m$^3$). Measurements were classified against WHO 2021 Air Quality Guidelines: annual guideline 5~µg/m$^3$; 24-hour guideline 15~µg/m$^3$ (99th percentile)~\cite{who2021guidelines}.

Indoor school exposure was modeled using infiltration factors (indoor/outdoor ratios 0.50--0.80) derived from published Central Asian building stock literature~\cite{abt2000relative,macintosh2009residential,chen2012review}. School exposure windows were defined as 08:00--15:00 weekdays; commute windows as 07:00--09:00 and 16:00--18:00.

Seasonal classification followed calendar months: winter (December--February), spring (March--May), summer (June--August), fall (September--November).

\subsection{Health Impact Assessment}

Attributable respiratory cases were estimated using the population attributable fraction approach with Global Burden of Disease Integrated Exposure-Response (IER) functions~\cite{burnett2014integrated,cohen2017estimates}:

\begin{equation}
\text{AF} = \frac{RR^{(C-C_0)/10} - 1}{RR^{(C-C_0)/10}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $C$ = observed PM2.5 concentration, $C_0$ = counterfactual level (5~µg/m$^3$, WHO annual guideline), and $RR$ = relative risk per 10~µg/m$^3$ increment (1.08; 95\% CI: 1.04--1.12) for pediatric respiratory outcomes.

Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation (10,000 iterations; random seed=42) sampled from: RR (log-normal distribution based on 95\% CI); population (normal; mean 420,000, SD 20,000); baseline incidence (uniform; 8--15\%); exposure (normal; mean 37.8, SD 38.4~µg/m$^3$).

\subsection{Intervention Cost-Effectiveness}

HEPA filtration cost-effectiveness modeling assumed: 800 Tashkent schools; 25 classrooms/school; \$2,500 capital cost/classroom; 80\% particle removal efficiency~\cite{fisk2017association,qi2017large}; 5-year equipment life; 10\% annual maintenance. Annualized costs were compared with projected cases prevented. DALY estimates assumed 0.15 disability weight for respiratory symptoms over 7-day average duration.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Analyses used Python 3.12 with pandas 2.0, numpy 1.24, scipy 1.10, and matplotlib 3.7. Descriptive statistics included mean, median, SD, IQR, and percentiles. Seasonal comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis H-test ($\alpha$=0.05). Uncertainty intervals are 95\% credible intervals from Monte Carlo simulation unless otherwise specified.

\subsection{Ethics Statement}

This study used exclusively public environmental monitoring data from OpenAQ containing no human participants, personal information, or individual health records. The study qualifies for institutional review board exemption under 45 CFR 46.104(d) as research involving existing public data without personal identifiers.
